# How onpar is written

One story, one voice, every page. This file is the contract for the README, the
landing page, the docs site and every diagram caption. A page that does not
follow it is not finished.

## The story

A team pays for a closed model. They suspect an open one would do the job for a
fraction of the cost, and nobody can prove it, because benchmarks are someone
else's exam. onpar turns one week of their own traffic into a proof, a serving
config, and a cutover with a rollback. Seven stages. Each one can say no.

Every page is one scene of that story, told to the reader as the engineer on
that team. The reader's traffic is the protagonist. onpar is the instrument.

## The shape of a stage page

1. **The question the team asks.** One sentence. ("Is it good enough for our
   tickets?")
2. **What onpar answers, and how.** Two or three short paragraphs. The mechanism,
   not a feature list.
3. **The run.** The command, then its real output, captured from a real run on
   a named machine on a named date. Never typed, never trimmed to look better.
4. **When it says no.** The refusal this stage can make, shown as output.
5. **The knobs that matter.** Only the flags a reader will actually reach for.
6. **Next.** The one command that follows.

A diagram appears only where the mechanism is easier to see than to read, at
most one per page, with a caption that says what to look at.

## Voice

- Second person, present tense. "You point your base URL at the gateway."
- One idea per sentence. A verb in every sentence. Twenty words is long.
- Headings are sentences or questions, not labels. "Fit tells you what runs
  here" beats "Fit solver".
- No hype. Banned: world-class, next-gen, cutting-edge, powerful, seamless,
  simply, just, robust, leverage, unlock, supercharge, delightful.
- No "we". onpar does things; the reader does things.
- Numbers are computed by the code (say which command), pinned by a test, or
  dated with a source. An estimate is called an estimate in the same sentence.
- Say what it does not do on the page where a reader would expect it to.
- No self-flagellation and no changelog in the prose. Bugs that were fixed live
  in tests and ADRs, not on the page.
- Dashes: a hyphen in compounds, nothing else. A new sentence beats an em dash.

## Output blocks

- The command on its own line first, then a blank line, then the output.
- Output comes from `tools/story_run.sh`, which re-runs the whole loop and
  rewrites the transcripts the docs embed. Hand edits to a transcript are
  reverted by the next run.
- Paths are the reader's (`~/.onpar/…`), never the author's.

## Diagrams

Generated by `tools/diagrams.py`. Each one answers one question in its title.
Animated diagrams are inlined so they honour the page's theme and
`prefers-reduced-motion`. A diagram that needs more than one sentence of
caption to be understood is redrawn.

## Pages that are not stages

- **Start here** gets the reader to a real result in ten seconds (`fit`) and a
  real proof in ten minutes (the walkthrough).
- **For agents** is written to the agent: what it may call, what it may never
  do, and what each exit code means.
- **Reference** is generated: the CLI page from `--help`, the receipt page from
  the format module. Prose there is one line per entry.
- **Decisions** are the ADRs, verbatim.
- **Project** holds the specs that built the product (PRD, plan, GA readiness,
  landscape, pitch, naming). They are history and intent, kept out of the
  reader's path and never rewritten to match the present.
